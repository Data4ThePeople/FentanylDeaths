# Fentanyl in the United States: a short sourced history

Background research for the post on fentanyl deaths by county, 1999-2024.
Every source below was fetched and read on 2026-11-05 unless marked otherwise.
Numbers are quoted as each source states them. Nothing here asserts a cause
unless the source does, and then the source is named.

## 1. Origin and medical use

- Fentanyl "was first synthesized in Belgium in 1960 by Dr. Paul Janssen at Janssen Pharmaceutica" (review article). It was introduced as an IV pain drug in Europe in 1963 and in the United States in 1968. Source: Int J Mol Sci 2025, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765396/
- Another review says fentanyl became available in the US in 1968 "but only in combination with droperidol." Source: Ochsner J 2023, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10016219/
- DEA says fentanyl "was introduced into medical practice as an intravenous anesthetic under the trade name of Sublimaze in the 1960s." Source: DEA Diversion Control Division fact sheet (Dec. 2025), https://www.deadiversion.usdoj.gov/drug_chem_info/fentanyl.pdf
- FDA's drug database lists Sublimaze (fentanyl citrate injection), NDA 016619, with an original approval date of Feb. 19, 1968. Source: Drugs@FDA, https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/index.cfm?event=overview.process&ApplNo=016619
- FDA lists the Duragesic patch (Janssen), NDA 019813, as approved Aug. 7, 1990. Source: Drugs@FDA, https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/index.cfm?event=overview.process&ApplNo=019813
- Potency: DEA says fentanyl "is about 100 times more potent than morphine as an analgesic" (https://www.deadiversion.usdoj.gov/drug_chem_info/fentanyl.pdf). CDC says pharmaceutical fentanyl is "50 to 100 times more potent than morphine" (https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/about/fentanyl.html).
- CDC says pharmaceutical fentanyl comes as patches or lozenges, not pills, while illegally made fentanyl is most often a powder or pressed into fake pills. Source: https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/about/fentanyl.html

## 2. Earlier illicit episodes

- DEA says "Fentanyl misuse initially appeared in mid-1970s." Source: https://www.deadiversion.usdoj.gov/drug_chem_info/fentanyl.pdf
- A 1988 paper says the fentanyls "first appeared on the streets in California in 1979 under the name 'China White'," and that at least 10 analogs were thought to be responsible for over 100 overdose deaths. Source: Henderson GL, J Forensic Sci 1988, PMID 3286815, https://europepmc.org/article/MED/3286815
- CDC wrote in 2008 that "since the 1970s," non-pharmaceutical fentanyl and analogs such as alphamethylfentanyl have been made illegally, and that a 1980s epidemic caused at least 110 fatal overdoses from 10 fentanyl analogs. Source: MMWR 2008;57(29), https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm5729a1.htm
- 2005-2007 outbreak: a CDC/DEA system found 1,013 deaths tied to non-pharmaceutical fentanyl from April 4, 2005, to March 28, 2007, peaking at 150 in June 2006. The first alert came from Camden, N.J., in April 2006. Counts by area: Cook County, Ill., 349; Philadelphia, 269; Wayne County, Mich., 230; New Jersey, 86; St. Louis city and county, 60; Delaware, 19. Source: same MMWR, https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm5729a1.htm
- The same report says police closed a fentanyl production site in Toluca, Mexico, in May 2006. Source: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm5729a1.htm
- DEA says that after this outbreak it moved to control fentanyl precursor chemicals: NPP became a List I chemical in 2007 and ANPP a Schedule II immediate precursor in 2010. Source: https://www.deadiversion.usdoj.gov/drug_chem_info/fentanyl.pdf

## 3. CDC's three waves

- "The first wave began with increased prescribing of opioids in the 1990s," with prescription opioid deaths rising "starting around 1999."
- "The second wave began in 2010, with rapid increases in overdose deaths involving heroin."
- "The third wave began in 2013, with substantial increases in overdose deaths involving synthetic opioids, particularly those involving illegally made fentanyl and fentanyl analogs."
- Source for all three: CDC, https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/about/understanding-the-opioid-overdose-epidemic.html
- NCHS says the death rate for synthetic opioids other than methadone was "mostly stable" from 2003 (0.5 per 100,000) to 2013 (1.0), then reached 22.7 in 2022. Source: NCHS Data Brief 522, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db522.htm
- From 2014 to 2015, that death rate rose 72.2%. CDC said the rise was "likely driven primarily by illicitly manufactured fentanyl." Source: MMWR 2016;65(50-51), https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/65/wr/mm655051e1.htm

## 4. How illicit fentanyl spread (as DEA describes it)

- 2018 DEA testimony: illicit fentanyl, analogs and their precursors "are often produced in China," then shipped by mail or express carrier to the US or to criminal groups in Mexico, Canada and the Caribbean. There, fentanyl is prepared "for mixing into the heroin supply, other non-opioid drugs, or pressed into a tablet form." Some groups had pill presses shipped from China. Source: DEA statement of Paul E. Knierim, Oct. 2, 2018, https://www.justice.gov/file/1099336/download
- The same testimony says NFLIS labs reported 36,061 fentanyl reports in 2016, compared with 1,042 in 2013. Source: https://www.justice.gov/file/1099336/download
- 2023 DEA testimony: the Sinaloa and Jalisco cartels buy precursor chemicals in China, ship them to Mexico, make fentanyl, and press it into fake pills. "DEA lab testing reveals that 6 out of 10 of these fentanyl-laced fake prescription pills contain a potentially lethal dose." DEA seized more than 58 million fake pills in 2022. DEA launched "One Pill Can Kill" in 2021. Source: Anne Milgram statement, July 27, 2023, https://judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/republicans-judiciary.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/milgram-testimony.pdf
- DEA defines 2 mg of fentanyl as one potentially deadly dose. DEA says 29% of fentanyl pills tested in fiscal year 2025 held a potentially lethal dose, down from 76% "just two years ago." Fentanyl powder purity fell to 10.3% from 19.5% over the same period. DEA says these drops "correlate with" lower synthetic opioid deaths (DEA's claim, not CDC's). Source: DEA FY 2027 Performance Budget (April 2026), https://www.justice.gov/jmd/media/1434526/dl
- DEA says, citing its 2025 National Drug Threat Assessment, that Mexican criminal groups "have cemented their role in supplying fentanyl." In 2024 DEA seized 9,950 kg of fentanyl and 61.1 million fake pills, about 29% and 24% less than in 2023. Source: https://www.deadiversion.usdoj.gov/drug_chem_info/fentanyl.pdf
- Mixing: DEA says fentanyl "is often mixed with other drugs, such as heroin and cocaine," and that users may not know it is there. CDC says powdered fentanyl can be mixed with heroin, cocaine and methamphetamine. DEA also reports "a rise in illicit fentanyl mixed with xylazine." Sources: https://www.deadiversion.usdoj.gov/drug_chem_info/fentanyl.pdf, https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/about/fentanyl.html, https://www.justice.gov/jmd/media/1434526/dl
- NCHS literal-text data show methamphetamine was involved in 32.1% of overdose deaths in 2023, up from 13.3% in 2017. Source: NVSR 75(1), March 2026, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nvsr/nvsr75/nvsr75-01.pdf

## 5. Why T40.4 coding matters

- NCHS says ICD-10 codes "may reflect broad categories of drugs rather than unique or specific drugs. For example, fentanyl, Demerol, tramadol, nitazenes, and Suboxone are each classified to the same ICD-10 code T40.4, 'other synthetic narcotics'." Source: NVSR 75(1), https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nvsr/nvsr75/nvsr75-01.pdf
- NCHS data briefs define the category as including "fentanyl, fentanyl analogs, and tramadol." Source: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db549.htm
- An NCHS report calls the category one that includes fentanyl and fentanyl analogs "more importantly, but also, less significantly, tramadol and others." Source: NVSR 72(7), 2023, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nvsr/nvsr72/nvsr72-07.pdf
- How much of T40.4 is fentanyl: NCHS gives no single share. Its literal-text study shows fentanyl in 73,297 deaths in 2023 (68.9% of overdose deaths), while the tramadol death rate was 0.3 per 100,000 in 2023. Data Brief 549 counts 72,776 T40.4 deaths in 2023. These come from different methods and should not be divided into a share. Sources: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nvsr/nvsr75/nvsr75-01.pdf, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db549.htm
- DEA says its NFLIS lab database "does not distinguish between pharmaceutical and illicitly manufactured fentanyl." Source: https://www.deadiversion.usdoj.gov/drug_chem_info/fentanyl.pdf
- Unspecified drugs: the share of overdose deaths that named at least one specific drug was 75% to 79% from 1999 through 2013 (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db329.htm), then rose from 81% in 2014 to 96% in 2023 (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db522.htm). In 2015, 17% of overdose death certificates did not name the drugs involved (https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/65/wr/mm655051e1.htm). (Our inference, not an NCHS statement: early-year T40.4 counts likely miss some deaths, more so before 2014.)

## 6. Recent turn

- Final data: 79,384 overdose deaths in 2024 (23.1 per 100,000), down from 105,007 (31.3) in 2023. The rate fell 26.2%. Deaths involving synthetic opioids other than methadone fell 35.6%, from 22.2 to 14.3 per 100,000 (72,776 to 47,735 deaths). Source: NCHS Data Brief 549, Jan. 2026, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db549.htm
- NVSR literal-text data show fentanyl deaths rose from 27,542 in 2017 to 73,944 in 2022, then fell slightly to 73,297 in 2023. Source: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nvsr/nvsr75/nvsr75-01.pdf
- Provisional: NCHS estimated 80,391 deaths in 2024, down 26.9% from 110,037 in 2023, and did not give a cause. Source: NCHS release, May 14, 2025, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/pressroom/releases/20250514.html
- CDC's injury center said deaths "steadily declined each month" since late 2023 and called this "a strong sign that public health interventions are making a difference" (CDC's view). Source: May 14, 2025, https://www.cdc.gov/media/releases/2025/2025-statement-from-cdcs-national-center-for-injury-prevention-and-control-on-provisional-2024.html
- Provisional 2025: NCHS estimated 69,973 deaths, down almost 14% from 81,313 in 2024, with no cause given. Source: May 13, 2026, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/pressroom/releases/20260513.html
- DEA links the drop to lower pill potency and powder purity (see section 4). That is DEA's claim.

- That the 1979 California "China White" was alpha-methylfentanyl specifically: the Henderson abstract says only "the fentanyls"; the name comes from secondary sources.
