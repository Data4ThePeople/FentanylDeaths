# Beats: fentanyl deaths, county by county

Outline for Eric's draft. Numbers come from `scripts/story_numbers.py` (rerun it to check them), from the page data, and from `RESEARCH.md` and `RESEARCH-funding.md`, with sources linked. Beat 3 is Claude's draft, as Eric asked. The other beats are notes for Eric to write from.

"Fentanyl" below means CDC's T40.4 group, deaths involving synthetic opioids other than methadone, which is mostly illegally made fentanyl. One death can involve several drugs.

## Beat 1. Why fentanyl

Where this started: the SUD analysis from earlier this week.

- From 2013 to 2022, yearly overdose deaths in the U.S. went up by 63,959, from 43,982 to 107,941. Over the same years, deaths involving fentanyl went up by 70,733, from 3,105 to 73,838. Overdose deaths that did not involve fentanyl went down by 6,774. So the whole rise in those years was in deaths involving fentanyl.
- Over the longer span, 2001 to 2022, deaths involving fentanyl account for 82% of the rise in overdose deaths.
- Fentanyl's share of overdose deaths: 7% in 2013, 51% in 2019, 68% in 2022, 60% in 2024.
- The 2024 drop is also mostly fentanyl. Overdose deaths fell by 25,623 from 2023 to 2024, and deaths involving fentanyl fell by 25,041, which is 98% of the drop.
- Source: CDC WONDER, Multiple Cause of Death (the same data as the map), and SUD_V3's national overdose exports.
- Caution for the wording: "involving" fentanyl, not "caused by" fentanyl alone. Many of these deaths also involve other drugs.

## Beat 3. How fentanyl got here (Claude's draft, sourced)

Fentanyl started as a medicine. A Belgian chemist, Paul Janssen, first made it around 1960 ([review, Int J Mol Sci 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765396/)). The FDA approved it as an injected anesthetic called Sublimaze in 1968 ([Drugs@FDA](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/index.cfm?event=overview.process&ApplNo=016619)) and as the Duragesic skin patch in 1990 ([Drugs@FDA](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/index.cfm?event=overview.process&ApplNo=019813)). DEA says it is "approximately 100 times more potent than morphine and 50 times more potent than heroin" as a painkiller ([DEA](https://www.dea.gov/factsheets/fentanyl)).

Illegally made fentanyl showed up long before the recent crisis. In 1979, fentanyl-type drugs sold as "China White" appeared on the streets in California ([J Forensic Sci 1988](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/3286815)). From April 2005 to March 2007, CDC and DEA tied 1,013 deaths to illegally made fentanyl, most of them in the Chicago, Philadelphia and Detroit areas. Deaths peaked in June 2006, a month after police closed a fentanyl lab in Toluca, Mexico ([CDC MMWR 2008](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm5729a1.htm)). DEA then put limits on two chemicals used to make fentanyl, in 2007 and 2010 ([DEA](https://www.deadiversion.usdoj.gov/drug_chem_info/fentanyl.pdf)).

CDC describes the opioid crisis in three waves ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/about/understanding-the-opioid-overdose-epidemic.html)). The first began with more opioid prescribing in the 1990s. The second began in 2010 with a fast rise in heroin deaths. The third began in 2013, with deaths involving synthetic opioids, "particularly those involving illegally made fentanyl." The death rate for this group was about 1 per 100,000 in 2013 and 22.7 in 2022 ([NCHS Data Brief 522](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db522.htm)). From 2014 to 2015 alone it rose 72%, and CDC said that rise was "likely driven primarily by illicitly manufactured fentanyl" ([CDC MMWR 2016](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/65/wr/mm655051e1.htm)).

DEA has described how the supply worked. In 2018 it told Congress that fentanyl and the chemicals to make it were "often produced in China" and shipped by mail to the U.S., or to groups in Mexico, Canada and the Caribbean. There it was mixed into heroin and other drugs or pressed into pills ([DEA testimony, 2018](https://www.justice.gov/file/1099336/download)). Drug labs reported fentanyl 36,061 times in 2016, up from 1,042 in 2013 (same source). By 2023, DEA said the Sinaloa and Jalisco cartels bought the chemicals in China, made fentanyl in Mexico, and pressed it into fake pills that look like prescription drugs. It said 6 in 10 of the fake pills it tested held a potentially deadly dose ([DEA testimony, 2023](https://judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/republicans-judiciary.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/milgram-testimony.pdf)). DEA counts 2 milligrams as a potentially deadly dose ([DEA](https://www.dea.gov/onepill)).

Fentanyl is also mixed into other drugs. DEA says it "is often mixed with other drugs, such as heroin and cocaine," and that people may not know it is there ([DEA](https://www.deadiversion.usdoj.gov/drug_chem_info/fentanyl.pdf)). In 2025 DEA reported that fentanyl purity was going down, while fentanyl mixed with animal tranquilizers such as xylazine was on the rise ([DEA, May 2025](https://www.dea.gov/press-releases/2025/05/15/dea-releases-2025-national-drug-threat-assessment)).

Notes on this draft:
- "Around 1960": one source says 1959, the review says 1960.
- "50 times more potent than heroin" is a multiplier, which the style rules avoid. It is DEA's quote, so it stays as a quote or comes out.
- The 2005-2007 outbreak: CDC's report gives the lab closing (May 2006) and the peak (June 2006) but does not link them, so the draft gives both dates and no cause.

## Beat 4. The counties that stand out

Only counties with 20 or more deaths in a year count here, the same rule as the page's ranking. Small counties can post very high rates from a few deaths.

- **Southern West Virginia has the highest rates on record.** McDowell County, WV, had 206.4 deaths per 100,000 in 2023 (36 deaths), the highest county rate in any year. Ranking counties by their worst year, eight of the top ten are in West Virginia: McDowell, Wyoming, Logan, Mercer, Raleigh, Cabell, Mason and Fayette. The other two are Baltimore city and Scioto County, OH.
- **Baltimore city is the big-city outlier.** It had the highest rate in the country in 2024 (95.2, 541 deaths), and its peak was 135.6 in 2021 (782 deaths). It was in the national top 25 by rate every year from 2016 to 2024.
- **Every year in the top 25, 2016-2024:** Baltimore city, Cabell County, WV (Huntington), and St. Louis city, MO.
- **Outside the East:** Rio Arriba County, NM, had the second-highest rate in 2024 (80.1, 32 deaths). Anchorage, AK, was 7th (55.9, 162 deaths).
- **The largest counts are in big counties with lower rates.** 2024: Maricopa County, AZ, 1,005 deaths (21.5 per 100,000); Cook County, IL, 990 (19.1); Los Angeles County, CA, 988 (10.1).

## Beat 5. 2024: the drop, who bucked it, and what may explain it

**The drop.** Deaths involving fentanyl fell from 72,776 in 2023 to 47,735 in 2024. The rate fell 35.6%, the most of any drug group ([NCHS Data Brief 549](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db549.htm)).

**Every state fell.** All 49 states (counting DC) with numbers shown for both years had fewer deaths in 2024. Hawaii (2024) and New Hampshire (2023) have totals withheld to protect small county counts. The biggest drops were West Virginia (-48.4%), Arkansas (-46.6%), North Dakota (-46.1%), Virginia (-45.8%) and Wisconsin (-45.7%). The smallest were in the West: Nevada (-4.9%), Alaska (-6.9%), Utah (-9.8%), Washington (-13.5%) and Arizona (-16.5%).

**Nearly every county fell too.** Of 435 counties with 20 or more deaths in both 2023 and 2024, 411 (94.5%) had fewer deaths in 2024, 5 were unchanged and 19 rose. With a lower bar, 10 or more deaths in both years, 639 of 706 counties (90.5%) fell.

**It was not a change in how deaths were recorded.** All overdose deaths fell 26%, whatever drug was named. Overdose deaths where the drug was not named also fell, from 39,160 to 29,771, so fentanyl deaths were not moved into "unspecified."

**The drop was almost all in fentanyl.** Deaths involving fentanyl fell 34.4% (72,776 to 47,735). Overdose deaths without fentanyl fell 1.8% (32,231 to 31,649).

**The counties that went the other way.** Of 435 counties with 20 or more deaths in both 2023 and 2024, 19 had more deaths in 2024. Nine of them are in Western states, three in Washington.
- Spokane County, WA: 176 to 249 deaths (31.9 to 44.8 per 100,000), the largest increase.
- Yakima County, WA: 87 to 111. Cowlitz County, WA: 35 to 40.
- Clark County, NV (Las Vegas): 493 to 519.
- Mohave County, AZ: 40 to 53. Matanuska-Susitna Borough, AK: 32 to 43. Monterey County, CA: 45 to 57.
- Outside the West: Richland County, OH (35 to 43), Wayne County, IN (24 to 29), Aroostook County, ME (28 to 31).
- Full list of 19 in `scripts/story_numbers.py` output.

**What may explain the drop** (from `RESEARCH-funding.md`; none of these sources measures what each factor contributed):
- CDC named naloxone, better access to treatment, changes in the drug supply, and prevention programs such as Overdose Data to Action as possible reasons. It did not rank them ([CDC, Feb 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/media/releases/2025/2025-cdc-reports-decline-in-us-drug-overdose-deaths.html)).
- A *Science* paper argues a 2023 disruption in the fentanyl supply was the likely main driver ([UMD summary](https://today.umd.edu/study-global-fentanyl-supply-disruption-led-to-drop-in-opioid-overdose-deaths)).
- A *JAMA Health Forum* study tied about 9% of the drop to fentanyl's falling share of drug seizures ([study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12811803/)).
- A *JAMA* study found naloxone was given at about the same rate per overdose as before the drop ([Brown](https://www.brown.edu/news/2026-10-21/naloxone-overdose-deaths-trend)).
- DEA reports lower fentanyl purity and fewer pills with a deadly dose: 29% in fiscal 2025, down from 76% two years earlier ([DEA budget](https://www.justice.gov/jmd/media/1434526/dl)).
- A preprint using the same WONDER data found the same split: fentanyl deaths fell sharply while other drug deaths barely moved. The author says this fits a change in the fentanyl supply better than broad program effects ([Busch, medRxiv, not peer-reviewed](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2025.12.04.25341579.full.pdf)).
- A peer-reviewed paper pushes back: national fentanyl purity "did not track fatal fentanyl overdose rates in most United States regions," so purity alone does not explain the drop ([Science Advances, via USC Schaeffer](https://schaeffer.usc.edu/research/fentanyl-purity-and-overdose-decline-a-reexamination-of-geographic-trends/)).
- Where this leaves us: the evidence leans toward the drug supply, and no study measures how much programs added. The cause is not settled.
- Possible tie-in to the West: DEA's warning about fentanyl mixed with other drugs. No source here explains why the West fell less, so this would be a question, not a claim.

## Beat 6. Looking ahead: funding and the next data

**Eric's angle: the unknown is the risk.** Deaths fell fast, and the people who study it do not agree on why. If the drop came mostly from the drug supply, it could reverse if the supply changes. Cutting programs on the belief that the problem is solved would remove support at the moment no one can say what is holding deaths down. (Eric's argument. The sources below support the pieces of it; none of them predicts a rebound.)

What backs each piece:
- The cause is not settled: see beat 5 (*Science*, preprint, *Science Advances*, CDC's unranked list).
- The decline may not last: the *Science* authors write "How long the decline will last is uncertain" and that "Treatment, prevention and harm-reduction efforts remain essential" ([UMD summary](https://today.umd.edu/study-global-fentanyl-supply-disruption-led-to-drop-in-opioid-overdose-deaths)).
- The supply is still changing: DEA warns that medetomidine, "a more powerful veterinary anesthetic," has emerged in the fentanyl supply, and that mixing fentanyl with animal tranquilizers is "on the rise" ([DEA, May 2025](https://www.dea.gov/press-releases/2025/05/15/dea-releases-2025-national-drug-threat-assessment)).
- Deaths are still high: the provisional count is 67,798 overdose deaths in the 12 months ending March 2026 ([CDC, Aug 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/media/releases/2026/cdc-launches-new-overdose-prevention-data-channel.html)).
- Funding is in play: the proposed and enacted changes below.

Wording guardrails for this beat:
- "Could come back" is fine. "Will come back" or "easily" goes past what the sources show.
- Stick to what is proposed or enacted. The argument is about how the system decides funding, not about a party or a person.
- "No study measures what the programs added" is accurate. "The programs did nothing" is not shown either.

**What Congress has done (enacted):**
- FY2026 funding held. State Opioid Response is $1.595 billion and the substance use block grant is $2.013 billion. Congress did not adopt the plan to merge them ([GHAI table](https://assets.advocacyincubator.org/uploads/2026/FY-2027-Appropriations-Crosswalk-SAMHSA-Funding-1.pdf)).
- FY2027 is not passed. Programs run at FY2026 levels through December 11, 2026 ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R49344.html)).

**What is proposed (HHS FY2027 budget request, [HHS Budget in Brief](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/fy-2027-budget-in-brief.pdf)):**
- State Opioid Response and both block grants merged into one $4.621 billion block grant, the same total as today.
- Substance abuse prevention cut from $350 million to $89 million; treatment programs from $564 million to $490 million.
- CDC's injury center, which runs the overdose programs, moved to a new agency at $588 million, down from $761 million.
- SAMHSA folded into the proposed Administration for a Healthy America.

**Grants that were cut and then restored (reported):** about $2 billion in SAMHSA grants ended in January 2026 and were reinstated the next evening ([NACo](https://www.counties.org/news-and-media-article/samhsa-terminates-2-billion-grant-funding-quickly-rescinds-terminations/)). About $140 million in Overdose Data to Action money was held back in mid-2025 and later expected to be paid in full ([NPR via KAXE](https://www.kaxe.org/news/2025-08-05/cdc-to-disburse-delayed-funds-for-fighting-fentanyl-and-more-staffers-say)).

**Medicaid (enacted July 2025):** work requirements and eligibility checks every six months begin around the end of 2026, with exemptions for people in substance use treatment ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/health-provisions-in-the-2025-federal-budget-reconciliation-law/)). Medicaid covers about one-fifth of adults with a substance use disorder ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/implications-of-medicaid-work-and-reporting-requirements-for-adults-with-mental-health-or-substance-use-disorders)).

**The next data, and the promise to update:**
- 2024 final data were added to CDC WONDER in February 2026 ([WONDER What's New](https://wonder.cdc.gov/whatsnew.html)). CDC has not announced a date for 2025 final data. If it follows last year's timing, that would be early 2027.
- An early look: CDC's provisional count is 69,973 overdose deaths in 2025, down almost 14% from 2024. That count does not break out fentanyl ([NCHS, May 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/pressroom/releases/20260513.html)).
- Close with the promise to update the map when the 2025 final county data come out.
